## Notes on adoption

These notes are intended for the project maintainers, not for
contributors. They should be removed from the version that contributors
see.

### Why a CLA at all?

Three reasons:

1. **Dual-licensing.** Without a CLA, every contributor whose code
   remains in the tree must be individually contacted and must
   individually consent before any commercial-license transaction can
   close. A CLA grants Cinteraction the sublicensing right up front, so
   commercial licensing becomes a routine operation rather than a
   coordination problem.

2. **License changes.** If, at some future point, the project decides
   to relicense (for example, to add an AGPL-3.0 variant for cloud
   deployments, or to upgrade to GPL-4.0 if and when it is published),
   the sublicensing right makes this possible without a per-contributor
   consent campaign.

3. **Defensive patent grants.** The patent-licence clauses in Sections 3
   of both parts protect the project and its users against patent
   litigation by contributors. The mutual-termination clause is a
   reciprocity mechanism: if a contributor sues someone over a patent
   that reads on the project, they lose their own patent licence.

### Alternatives

A **Developer Certificate of Origin (DCO)** is a lighter-weight
alternative: contributors add `Signed-off-by: Name <email>` to each
commit, asserting that they have the right to submit the work but not
granting any sublicensing rights. The Linux kernel and many other large
projects use DCO. If Cinteraction decides not to pursue commercial
licensing or relicensing, DCO would be sufficient and is much less
friction for contributors.

The choice between CLA and DCO is largely a business decision:
**CLA preserves dual-licensing optionality; DCO does not**. If
dual-licensing is part of the business model (as discussed in the
Section 6 paragraph of the companion paper), the CLA is the right
choice. If it is not, DCO is.

### Signing mechanisms

In rough order of friction (lowest to highest):

1. **CLA-assistant bot on GitHub** ([cla-assistant.io](https://cla-assistant.io)).
   First-time contributor opens a pull request, the bot posts a comment
   with a "click to sign" link, the contributor clicks through, and the
   pull request becomes eligible to merge. Signature record stored in a
   GitHub repository. This is what most modern projects use.

2. **EasyCLA** (managed by the Linux Foundation). More features
   (corporate manager dashboards, multiple projects), more setup.

3. **Out-of-band signature** (PDF returned by email, DocuSign). Necessary
   for corporations whose legal teams require a wet or e-signed
   document; the CLA-assistant flow may be insufficient for them.

A practical pattern is to use option 1 by default and offer option 3
on request for contributors whose employers require it.

### Things this template does *not* cover

- **Trademark.** "Cinteraction" and "IVCT" are presumably trademarks
  (registered or not) of Cinteraction d.o.o. A separate trademark
  policy document — describing what derivative projects may and may not
  call themselves — should be drafted alongside this CLA. Without one,
  there is no clear answer to "can a fork call itself IVCT-Lite?"

- **Trade secrets.** If any part of the framework contains material
  that Cinteraction treats as a trade secret, that material should not
  be released under GPL-3.0 in the first place — releasing it is itself
  an irreversible loss of trade secret status. A confidentiality
  review of the codebase before public release is wise.

- **Export controls.** WebRTC and AI software may be subject to
  export-control regulations in some jurisdictions. Counsel should
  confirm whether IVCT in its full form is subject to any such
  restrictions before release.
